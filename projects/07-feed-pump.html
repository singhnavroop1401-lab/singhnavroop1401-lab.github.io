Reciprocating Feed Pump

Seventeen parts modelled from flat views and constrained into one assembly that moves the way the real thing moves. Five of them were not in the brief — they had to be read out of the geometry of the parts that were.

Context
CAD practical — B.Eng. Mechanical Engineering, Thapar Institute
Brief
Build a reciprocating feed pump from the supplied 2-D part views, then assemble it with constraints that leave the right degrees of freedom open.
Tool
Onshape — parts, assembly, drawings
Scale
12 given parts · 5 inferred · one constrained assembly

Twelve parts from flat views

A feed pump raises the pressure of boiler feedwater enough for it to get into the boiler against its own steam. The reciprocating kind is the one you use when the flow is small: a plunger displaces a fixed volume per stroke, two non-return valves make sure it only goes one way, and an air vessel on the discharge damps the pulsing that a single plunger would otherwise send down the pipe.

The brief supplied 2-D views of twelve parts — barrel body, plunger, barrel liner, cover liner, barrel cover, chest cover, valve chest, valve seat, valve, air vessel, bracket, screw. Each had to be read off the views and rebuilt as a solid.

The five that weren't in the brief

The supplied set didn't close. Assembling the twelve leaves a gland with nothing in it and flanges with nothing holding them together — a stuffing box, two sizes of bolt, a nut and a washer are all needed and none were drawn. Their sizes came from what they had to fit: bolt diameters from the flange hole patterns already modelled, the stuffing box from the bore it seals against and the depth left between the cover and the barrel.

This is the part of the exercise worth keeping. Modelling a part from a dimensioned view is transcription; sizing one from the space it has to occupy is the actual skill, and it is the one that transfers to a real drawing set where something is always missing.

Assembly, and the motion it has to allow

Constraining an assembly is a set of decisions about what should still be able to move. Here the plunger slides in the barrel body, the valves lift off their seats and drop back, and the screws turn. Everything else is fixed. Getting that right is what separates an assembly from a pile of solids sharing a coordinate system.

What I'd do differently

  • The plunger fit is modelled as nominal-to-nominal. Solid on solid, zero clearance — the one fit in the pump that cannot be zero. A plunger needs a running clearance and a packed gland; modelled tight like this it would seize, and modelled loose it would leak. The geometry is right and the engineering of it is missing.
  • Five parts inferred is five assumptions. The stuffing box in particular is one reading of the space. Packing depth and gland travel aren't pinned down by the mating faces, so a different depth would close the assembly just as well and pack differently. I should have written down which dimensions were driven and which I chose.
  • Nothing was swept. The constraints demonstrate that the plunger can slide and the valves can lift, but no motion study runs them through a full stroke. Interference that only appears at mid-lift would not have been caught.
  • No materials, so no duty. Without densities the assembly has no mass, and without mass there is nothing to check a drive against — no stroke volume, no delivery rate, no idea what torque it takes to turn. That gap is exactly what the drive assembly project had to fill in afterwards.
